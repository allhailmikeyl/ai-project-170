You Rice Me Up: Predicting Philippine Rice Price Movements from Weather

TL;DR

We ask whether a city’s monthly weather helps predict if its retail rice price will go up or down next month. We implement kNN, Gaussian Naive Bayes, and a perceptron from scratch, and run each on price-only, weather-only, and price-plus-weather features to see how much the weather adds.

Problem Statement and Motivation

Rice is a foundational staple food in the Philippines, and fluctuations of its market price directly impact general household food security, farmer livelihoods, and overall economic stability (Cororaton et al., 2018). The domestic price of rice in the country is highly sensitive to the amount of crop supply available. As such, the country’s severe exposure to climate variability and its extreme climatic conditions serve as frequent triggers of price volatility (Nguyen et al., 2024; Stuecker et al., 2018). Considering the agricultural structure and landscape of the country, being able to anticipate such changes serves as a boon not only for farmers and consumers, but also for policymakers and local government units. This presents the gap in being able to assess whether local meteorological conditions can act as a predictive signal for the price movements of rice at the city level.

A related issue lies in how the localized nature of both weather events and market dynamics across the archipelago. This results in distinct price spreads caused by different regional yields and market segmentation (Diaz, 2025). The traditional methods used in price forecasting mostly utilize historical price time-series data. While this correctly captures past market momentum, it fails to account for and anticipate incoming supply disruptions caused by changes in the local climate. Although other macroeconomic and agricultural studies discuss the relation between local climate and crop yields, this study seeks to identify whether integrating local monthly weather variables is able to improve directional price prediction compared to traditional models.

Specifically, this study aims to:

  1. implement machine learning models capable of predicting the directional movement (increase, decrease) of the price of certain rice varieties;
  2. evaluate the prediction accuracy of local monthly meteorological data in directional prediction; and
  3. compare the performance of the implemented machine learning architectures in predicting short-term directional price accuracy across different cities and rice varieties.

The significance of the study lies in addressing the gap related to short-term retail price forecasting using localized weather data. By conducting empirical testing of correlation between city-level weather data and the effects it has on prediction power, the study establishes relevant insight for developing improved and more reliable agricultural and economic forecasting tools. Lastly, establishing a model(s) capable of reliably predicting directional changes in rice retail price allows the use of its functions to support and empower the involved members of the community.

Dataset

The dataset used in this project is made of two public datasets that the group combined by city and month for 2020 to 2023: monthly retail prices from PSA OpenSTAT and daily weather data from Kaggle. The combined dataset has 16 columns and 6,576 data rows, with records for different cities and months. Its features are the prices of three (3) types of rice, namely regular-milled, well-milled, and special, and nine (9) weather measurements: mean, maximum, and minimum temperature; precipitation amount and hours; maximum wind speed and gusts; shortwave radiation; and FAO evapotranspiration. The target is a label of 1 if a rice variety’s price is higher in the next month and 0 if it is lower or unchanged. Each rice variety is modeled separately.

Dataset Source What it contains
Retail prices of rice PSA OpenSTAT (Cereals retail price table, 2018-based) Monthly retail prices per kg of three rice varieties (well-milled, regular-milled, and special) for 119 geolocations
Philippine Cities Weather Data (2020-2023) Kaggle (daily_data_combined_2020_to_2023.csv) Daily weather records for 137 cities (over 200,000 rows), aggregated to monthly values

Task: for a given city and month, predict whether the retail price of rice goes up in the following month.

Preprocessing

In the preprocessing step, the two datasets were aligned by time and location. Daily weather observations were aggregated into monthly measurements to match the monthly rice price data. Moreover, rice prices are reported per province, so each city was assigned the price of the province it belongs to. The result is a dataset of city-month records that pair each city’s monthly weather data with the monthly rice price. All preprocessing was done in Google Sheets with formulas that reference the raw price and weather sheets, so every step can be traced back to the source data. The steps are detailed below.

  1. Weather, daily to monthly. For each city, the daily values in a month are turned into one monthly value. Daily mean temperatures and daily maximum wind speeds are averaged. The highest daily maximum temperature and lowest daily minimum temperature are, respectively, used for the maximum and minimum temperatures. Daily precipitation, precipitation hours, shortwave radiation, and FAO evapotranspiration values are summed to obtain their monthly totals. Lastly, for maximum wind gusts, the highest daily maximum wind gust value within the month is used.
  2. Location, city to province. Prices are reported per province, so each city is matched to its province (for example, Bacoor to Cavite and Calamba to Laguna). All NCR cities share the NCR price. City names that appear in more than one province (San Carlos, Talisay, and Isabela, for example) are assigned to the most likely province and noted as an assumption. 29 of the 82 provinces (including NCR) have no city with weather data and are left out, which leaves 52 provinces plus NCR.
  3. Price, province to city. Every city takes the price of its province. Cities stay as separate rows instead of being merged, because two cities in the same province can have very different weather even when their price is the same.
  4. Result. 137 cities over 48 months give 6,576 city-month rows.

Methods

All three methods are implemented from scratch with the Python standard library, and each solves the same task on the same data. They are meant to be meaningfully different: kNN is distance-based, Naive Bayes is probabilistic, and the perceptron is a linear classifier. All of them read the same standardized features, split, and scoring from one shared module, so the comparison is fair.

k-Nearest Neighbors

Predicts whether the next month's rice price will increase or not by comparing a city-month record with similar records from the training dataset. We implemented it using Euclidean distance (Minkowski distance with p = 2) on standardized features. It finds the k nearest records and uses majority voting to determine the prediction. We test different values of k and select the one with the highest validation macro-F1 score.

Gaussian Naive Bayes

A probabilistic classifier that predicts whether the next month's rice price will increase by calculating the probability of each possible class based on the input features. We use Gaussian distributions to model the features, assuming that they are independent of one another given the class. The algorithm calculates the prior probability of each class and the mean and standard deviation of each feature, then compares the log probability scores to determine the predicted class.

Perceptron

A linear classifier that uses a weighted sum of the input features and a bias term to predict whether the next month's rice price will increase. It uses a threshold function to classify the result. Whenever a record is misclassified, the weights are updated using the learning rate and the difference between the actual and predicted output. The weights start at zero, and the training rows are shuffled each epoch using a fixed seed of 170. We try learning rates of 0.001, 0.01, and 0.1 and epoch limits of 10, 50, and 100, and select the setting with the best validation macro-F1. Training stops at the epoch limit, or earlier if an epoch has no mistakes.

Experimental Setup

Data splits

We split by time instead of randomly, for two reasons. It matches the real task, which is forecasting forward from past data. It also prevents leakage: cities in the same province share identical prices and labels, so a random split would put near-copies of test rows in the training set. Features are standardized with the mean and standard deviation of the training rows only.

Part Feature months Rows

December 2021 is left out of the tuning split for the same reason as December 2022: its label comes from the first month of the next period.

Class balance. “Up” is 43 to 48% of the training labels and 47 to 54% of the 2022 validation labels, but 68 to 74% of the 2023 test labels, depending on the variety (regular-milled: 48%, then 54%, then 74%). Because of this shift, we use macro-F1 as the main metric and compare against baselines.

Metrics

  • Confusion matrix (TP, FP, TN, FN, with “up” as the positive class): shows what kind of mistakes a method makes, and every other metric is computed from it.
  • Accuracy: easy to read, but misleading here, since always predicting “up” would score 68 to 74% on the test year.
  • Macro precision and recall: show whether a method favors one class.
  • Macro-F1 (main metric): averages the F1 of both classes equally, so a method cannot score well by favoring the majority class.

Baselines. Majority class always predicts the more common training label. Persistence predicts “up” if the price already rose from last month to this month.

Hyperparameters

Each method is tuned the same way: train on 2020 to 2021, score on 2022, and keep the setting with the best macro-F1 (a tie keeps the earlier setting). The method is then retrained on 2020 to 2022 and scored once on 2023.

Reproducibility

The methods use only the Python standard library (matplotlib is used only to draw the charts), and there is no hidden randomness: anything random uses the fixed seed 170. Every method runs with the identical data, split, scaling, and metrics.

Discussion

k-Nearest Neighbors

kNN did not beat the persistence baseline in any configuration. Its test macro-F1 stayed between 0.450 and 0.460, compared with 0.498 to 0.517 for persistence, and adding weather changed it by less than 0.01. The tuning results also point to a weak signal. With weather features, k = 1 was usually chosen and validation scores fell as k grew, which means averaging more weather-similar months did not help. The test results were lowered further by a shift in class balance, since “up” made up 43 to 48% of the training labels but 68 to 74% of the 2023 labels (Lipton et al., 2018). kNN followed the training majority and predicted “up” too rarely (only 27% of the well-milled price-only test rows), so it missed most of the actual rises. In the price-plus-weather set, nine of the eleven features are weather, which can also weaken the distance calculation when features carry little information (Halder et al., 2024).
