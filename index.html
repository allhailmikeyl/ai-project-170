TL;DR

We ask whether a city’s monthly weather helps predict if its retail rice price will go up or down next month. We implement kNN, Gaussian Naive Bayes, and a perceptron from scratch, and run each on price-only, weather-only, and price-plus-weather features to see how much the weather adds.

Dataset

We combine two public datasets, joined by city and month for 2020 to 2023.

DatasetSourceWhat it contains
Retail prices of rice PSA OpenSTAT (Cereals retail price table, 2018-based) Monthly retail prices per kg of three rice varieties (well-milled, regular-milled, and special) for 119 geolocations
Philippine Cities Weather Data (2020-2023) Kaggle (daily_data_combined_2020_to_2023.csv) Daily weather records for 137 cities (over 200,000 rows), aggregated to monthly values

Task: for a given city and month, predict whether the retail price of rice goes up in the following month.

Preprocessing

All preprocessing was done in Google Sheets with formulas that reference the raw price and weather sheets, so every step can be traced back to the source data.

  1. Weather, daily to monthly. For each city, the daily values in a month are turned into one monthly value. Temperature, daily maximum wind speed, and shortwave radiation are averaged, while precipitation and precipitation hours are summed.
  2. Location, city to province. Prices are reported per province, so each city is matched to its province (for example, Bacoor to Cavite and Calamba to Laguna). All NCR cities share the NCR price. City names that appear in more than one province (San Carlos, Talisay, and Isabela, for example) are assigned to the most likely province and noted as an assumption. 29 of the 82 provinces (including NCR) have no city with weather data and are left out, which leaves 52 provinces plus NCR.
  3. Price, province to city. Every city takes the price of its province. Cities stay as separate rows instead of being merged, because two cities in the same province can have very different weather even when their price is the same.
  4. Result. 137 cities over 48 months give 6,576 city-month rows.

Methods

All three methods are implemented from scratch with the Python standard library, and each solves the same task on the same data. They are meant to be meaningfully different: kNN is distance-based, Naive Bayes is probabilistic, and the perceptron is a linear classifier. All of them read the same standardized features, split, and scoring from one shared module, so the comparison is fair.

k-Nearest Neighbors

Classifies a city-month by the majority label among its k closest training records, using Euclidean distance on the standardized features. A tie (even k) goes to the closest neighbor. k is chosen on the validation year from a list of odd values.

Gaussian Naive Bayes

Applies Bayes’ rule with the assumption that features are independent given the class. Each feature gets a per-class Gaussian learned from the training rows, the prior of a class is its share of the training rows, and the classes are compared by their log scores. A small variance floor stops a constant feature from dividing by zero.

Perceptron

A linear classifier with a bias weight. It goes through the training rows in order and, whenever a row is misclassified, updates the weights with w = w + r(d − y)x. Training stops after an epoch with no mistakes or at the epoch limit. The learning rate and the epoch limit are chosen on the validation year.

Experimental Setup

Data splits

We split by time instead of randomly, for two reasons. It matches the real task, which is forecasting forward from past data. It also prevents leakage: cities in the same province share identical prices and labels, so a random split would put near-copies of test rows in the training set. Features are standardized with the mean and standard deviation of the training rows only.

December 2021 is left out of the tuning split for the same reason as December 2022: its label comes from the first month of the next period.

Class balance. “Up” is 43 to 48% of the training labels and 47 to 54% of the 2022 validation labels, but 68 to 74% of the 2023 test labels, depending on the variety (regular-milled: 48%, then 54%, then 74%). Because of this shift, we use macro-F1 as the main metric and compare against baselines.

Metrics

  • Confusion matrix (TP, FP, TN, FN, with “up” as the positive class): shows what kind of mistakes a method makes, and every other metric is computed from it.
  • Accuracy: easy to read, but misleading here, since always predicting “up” would score 68 to 74% on the test year.
  • Macro precision and recall: show whether a method favors one class.
  • Macro-F1 (main metric): averages the F1 of both classes equally, so a method cannot score well by favoring the majority class.

Baselines. Majority class always predicts the more common training label. Persistence predicts “up” if the price already rose from last month to this month.

Hyperparameters

Each method is tuned the same way: train on 2020 to 2021, score on 2022, and keep the setting with the best macro-F1 (a tie keeps the earlier setting). The method is then retrained on 2020 to 2022 and scored once on 2023.

Reproducibility

The methods use only the Python standard library (matplotlib is used only to draw the charts), and there is no hidden randomness: anything random uses the fixed seed 170. Every method runs with the identical data, split, scaling, and metrics.
